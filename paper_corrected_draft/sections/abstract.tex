Supplying designated gold evidence is widely assumed to make language-model fact verification more reliable.
We test that assumption in a paired experiment on 10{,}000 balanced FEVER Supported/Refuted claims, evaluating GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-mini without evidence and with FEVER-designated gold evidence (40{,}000 predictions).
Evidence raises aggregate accuracy from 88.69\% to 96.04\% ($+7.35$~pp) for GPT-5.4 and from 84.67\% to 95.54\% ($+10.87$~pp) for GPT-5.4-mini.
Those gains conceal 226 unique claims that were correct without evidence and become wrong after the same gold evidence is supplied (40 shared by both models, 186 specific to one).
We describe these regressions with blinded five-judge progressive-disclosure adjudication by two model families, Grok and Claude, after repairing a structured-evidence stage whose historical inputs displayed corrupted sentence text.
A descriptive taxonomy keeps judge decisiveness and agreement with FEVER separate.
On the repaired inputs, the panels reach the FEVER label from the evidence sentences alone for 34.1\% (Grok) and 46.9\% (Claude) of regressions, reach a decisive verdict opposite to FEVER for 19.5\% and 23.9\%, and remain nondecisive for 31.4\% and 18.1\%.
Claude is more decisive than Grok at every disclosure stage; titles and structured evidence resolve a minority of nondecisive cases and never switch a decisive verdict to the opposite label; and exact per-claim category agreement between families is 76.1\% ($\kappa=0.67$).
The structured-stage analysis follows a dated amendment, written after judging, that analyses each job's accepted output; the results are stable when earlier valid outputs are substituted.
Evidence can raise overall accuracy while creating localized failures, and how those failures distribute across adjudication outcomes depends on the adjudicating model family; the outcomes do not identify why GPT changed its answers.
